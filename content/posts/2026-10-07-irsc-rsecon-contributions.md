September was a busy month for the international research software community. The first [International Research Software Conference (IRSC)](https://www.researchsoft.org/irsc/) and the 10th [Research Software Engineering Conference (RSECon26)](https://rsecon26.society-rse.org) took place in Sheffield, UK, bringing together research software engineers, advocates, policy makers and other members of the community from around the world.   

The Netherlands was well represented at both conferences. Members of the NL-RSE community contributed talks, workshops and Birds of a Feather sessions on topics ranging from research software sustainability and AI to training, reproducibility, impact and community building.   

We are happy to highlight the contributions from colleagues in the Netherlands below.   

*Were you at IRSC or RSECon26 and did we miss your contribution? Get in touch via [_info@nl-rse.org_](mailto:info@nl-rse.org) and let us know so we can add it to the list!*   

## Contributions at IRSC

### International Perspectives on Sustainability challenges and opportunities in research software training networks
*Contributors: Eva Lekkerkerker and Fenne Riemslagh, Netherlands eScience Center*   
[Full abstract](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1drzcAIY2yWg6GGY3nmEnO1mWg1a4QCp77XALlkp8L2c/edit?tab=t.0).   

Research software training is essential for the future of digital research, but training provision can still be fragmented and dependent on individual effort. This session brought together international perspectives on how to build sustainable training networks, support trainers, and develop clearer career and skills pathways for research software professionals.   


### Sustaining research software under AI assisted development 
*Contributors: Colette Bos and Faruk Diblen, Netherlands eScience Center*   
[Full abstract](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1KlzgpzcSXJweKCexYXC2y6aUYZ-eJ4xwr86xgu1SPus/edit?tab=t.0)   

How is generative AI changing the way research software is developed, and what does this mean for sustaining software over the long term? This session explored these questions and gathered perspectives on the changing roles of people involved in research software, arguing for disciplined coordination, AI provenance and careful human verification.   

### Committing to Sustainable Research Software with ADORE
*Contributors: Lieke de Boer, ZonMw; Marta Teperek, NWO; and Carlos Martinez-Ortiz, Netherlands eScience Center*   
[Full abstract](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1yjLR9ubQI6TFzdGPDobPhq51AZC2TFVCT45SbneCH7Y/edit?tab=t.0)    

The Amsterdam Declaration on Openness of Research Software (ADORE) aims to improve funding practices and support sustainable research software. This Birds of a Feather session brought together signatories, prospective adopters and others interested in cultural change to share experiences, discuss practical benefits and explore how ADORE can help organisations strengthen their research software practices.

### Convincing the Decision-Makers: Lessons from a Bottom-Up RSE Initiative in the Netherlands
*Contributors: Lieke de Boer, ZonMw; Pamela Wochner, TU Delft; and Carlos Martinez-Ortiz, Netherlands eScience Center*   
[Full abstract](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1QR3e6D_O7wU_ZsnvuyGRv05ZHen6WvZpz4nrLfZICyo/edit?tab=t.0#heading=h.rxmb6xr2uekp)   

What can research software professionals do when the policies and funding structures around them are not working? Drawing on a Dutch case where funding rules created barriers to sustainable RSE positions, this session shared lessons on identifying decision-makers, building support and bringing structural issues onto the policy agenda.

### Designing Impactful Hackathons for Research Software Communities
*Contributor: Wangyiyao Zhou, Eindhoven University of Technology*   
[Full abstract](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1QR3e6D_O7wU_ZsnvuyGRv05ZHen6WvZpz4nrLfZICyo/edit?tab=t.0#heading=h.rxmb6xr2uekp)   

Hackathons can support learning, software development and community building, but their impact depends strongly on how they are designed. This talk explored different ways of structuring hackathons to achieve different outcomes, and highlighted the importance of embedding these events within broader community and project frameworks.

### Lightning talk: What the Netherlands eScience center can provide to the International Research Software landscape
*Contributor: Carlos Martinez, Netherlands eScience Center*   

The Netherlands eScience Center supports researchers in the Netherlands through project calls, training and other activities, but many of the resulting practices and tools are relevant internationally. This lightning talk gave a brief overview of activities that could benefit the wider research software community and invited participants to continue the conversation.

### Lightning talk: Beyond Stars and Citations: Challenges of Finding Suitable Metrics for Research-Software Impact
*Contributor: Alexander Nolte, Eindhoven University of Technology*   

How do we measure the impact of research software? This lightning talk explored why traditional measures such as citations and software activity do not fully capture scientific value, sustainability or community uptake, and introduced open questions around developing more suitable ways to assess research-software impact.

### Research Software Metascience
*Contributor: Rena Bakhshi, Netherlands eScience Center & TNO*   
[Full abstract](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1g4kd0ifb0GmRybssyVWEHRGKbqyHW8JOv317X-lzfvk/edit?tab=t.0)   

Research Software Metascience examines how research software is created, used, shared, evaluated, funded and maintained, and how these practices affect research. This Birds of a Feather session explored the emerging field and its potential to strengthen collaboration between researchers while informing research software funding and assessment frameworks.

## Contributions at RSECon26

### RSE Worldwide session
*Contributor: Carlos Martinez Ortiz, Netherlands eScience Center (on behalf of NL-RSE)*   
[Full session details](https://rsecon26.society-rse.org/programme/rse-worldwide/)   

The RSE Worldwide session brings together representatives of RSE communities from different countries to exchange experiences, discuss challenges and strengthen international connections. Former NL-RSE core team member Carlos Martinez Ortiz represented the Netherlands in this global conversation, sharing the Dutch perspective alongside representatives from other established and emerging RSE communities.

### Hands-on with DashVERSE: Building and Customizing Research Software Quality Dashboards
*Contributor: Faruk Diblen, Netherlands eScience Center*   
[Full session details](https://virtual.oxfordabstracts.com/event/76908/submission/162)   

This hands-on workshop introduced DashVERSE, a customizable dashboard for monitoring and visualising research software quality, sustainability and compliance with best practices. Participants worked through the process of connecting software repositories, running automated assessments, interpreting quality metrics and customising dashboards for their own needs.

### Coding Confessions
*Contributor: Yasel Quintero, TU Delft and NL-RSE core team*   
[Full session details](https://virtual.oxfordabstracts.com/event/76908/submission/177)   

Coding Confessions creates a space for RSEs to talk openly about mistakes, failures and things they have learned along the way. NL-RSE core team member, Yasel Quintero, shared a personal experience from her work as an RSE at the TU Delft Digital Competence Centre, contributing to a session aimed at normalising failure and turning difficult experiences into opportunities for learning and discussion.

### Building a Reproducibility Check Service for Computational Research
*Contributors: Yasel Quintero and Joao Guimaraes, TU Delft Digital Competence Centre; Aleksandra Wilczynska, 4TU.ResearchData*   
[Full session details](https://virtual.oxfordabstracts.com/event/76908/submission/103)     

The TU Delft Digital Competence Centre and 4TU.ResearchData have developed a reproducibility check service that helps researchers test whether published computational results can be reproduced from their shared software and data. The contribution shared experiences from the pilot, including common challenges, ways of engaging researchers and opportunities to build communities of practice around reproducibility checking.
